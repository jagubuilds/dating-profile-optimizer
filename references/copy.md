# Writing prompts and bios

## Principles
- **Specific beats generic.** "Jazz on a weeknight at a tiny bar" beats "love live music". Specific details are believable and give people something to reply to; generic lines read as evasive.
- **Every line is a conversation hook.** Ask: can a stranger reply to this in one line? Questions, choices ("coffee or chai?"), mild challenges and small confessions all work.
- **Show, don't tell.** Don't write "I'm funny" - be funny. Don't write "I'm ambitious" - mention what they're building.
- **Balance edge with warmth.** Pure edge reads bitter; pure warmth reads bland. Pair a tease with a soft landing.
- **Understatement reads confident.** Say the big thing, then undercut it with a short dry tag ("Good at planning trips. Bad at packing for them.").
- **Truth check every line.** Mark anything you inferred with "only if true".

## Calibrate to the goal and the pool
| Goal / pool | How sharp to be |
|---|---|
| Most matches, or a small pool (small city, narrow age band, niche app) | Broad and warm. Specific details yes, polarising takes no. |
| Right people in a big pool | Filter on purpose: niche references and strong (low-stakes) opinions are features. |

"Rage-bait" only works on low-stakes topics (food, coffee, city debates, habits). Takes on gender, politics or religion get reports, not replies.

## Patterns that reliably get replies
- **The choice:** "X or Y? Choose wisely, it decides our first trip." They can answer in one word.
- **The game:** "Guess. Closest answer picks the date spot."
- **The running joke:** one self-aware theme repeated across a photo and one or two prompts (e.g. a self-confessed habit in a prompt + a photo that "proves" it). Use it twice at most, then show a different side.
- **Reverse psychology / warning label:** "Probably not your type. Unless your type builds things and texts back eventually." Works well on apps where users get a lot of attention.
- **Date built in:** "Instead of drinks, let's..." / "My ideal date involves..." - concrete and low-pressure.
- **Self-deprecating confidence:** "Serious about work, not serious about much else."

## Things to avoid
- Complaining about dating apps ("I hope this app is different...")
- "If we talk you'd know" and other non-answers
- Lists of adjectives, "partner in crime", "love to laugh"
- Jokes that don't involve the user (a generic meme about dating)
- Overtly marriage-minded framing (parents, setups by relatives, wedding jokes) when the user wants to reach a younger or casual-leaning audience
- Mentioning work too much if the user wants personality to lead - one light work signal is enough
- Anything that will be outdated (a deceased pet in present tense, a sports team "this season")

## Character limits
Limits differ by app and change over time (seen: 40 characters on some curated apps, 150 on Hinge, 160 on Bumble prompts). Ask the user for the counter shown in the app. When space is tight:
- Turn statements into games or choices
- Drop articles and filler; use full stops for rhythm ("Coffee first. Plans later.")
- Shorter synonyms: "chai or coffee" instead of "tea person or coffee person"
Always show the count next to each option.

## How to present options
Give 3-10 options per slot depending on how picky the user is, a ranked top 3, and one-line reasoning each. When the user rejects options, ask what felt off (too try-hard? too niche? not true?) and change direction rather than tweaking words.
